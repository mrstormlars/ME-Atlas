Methods

No medical or research expert has reviewed this content. The content, including every evidence level, is drafted, checked and decided by AI tools. No external medical or research reviewer has been recruited yet. Until one has, the grading rules below are applied as written, and they are a draft. The founder sets the rules and the direction and may overrule a decision as a logged correction.

What this site is

ME Atlas gives an overview of the serious {{ gloss("hypotheses") }} about what causes ME/CFS, and shows how much evidence stands behind each one. It describes the state of the evidence. It does not give treatment advice, and it does not say that an idea is right or wrong. Long COVID is marked on a hypothesis where the evidence overlaps. It is not a separate section.

Which hypotheses are listed

A hypothesis is listed when at least one {{ gloss("peer-reviewed") }} study has data from an ME/CFS group defined by a recognised {{ gloss("case definition") }}. The rule judges the method, not where an idea comes from. These are left out:

  • ideas with no peer-reviewed patient data yet;
  • studies of healthy people, animals or cells only (they can add background to a card, but cannot admit a hypothesis alone);
  • studies with no stated case definition cannot admit a hypothesis or count towards its level, because we cannot tell whether the patients had ME/CFS; a card may list them as other studies, for context;
  • claims backed only by opinion, anecdote, patient surveys without a clinical comparison, or material that is not peer-reviewed;
  • treatment products and commercial claims. The site lists hypotheses about causes, not remedies.

Hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. They may be links in one chain, or may apply to different groups of patients.

The evidence ladder

Draft — pending expert review. The bar for a hypothesis shows causal evidence, from rung 0 to rung 5. Grading follows only this published ladder; editors do not add criteria.

The six rungs
LevelNameWhat it means
0ProposedAn idea with a reasoned argument, but no direct patient data yet.
1First signsOne or more small or uncontrolled studies point towards it.
2Backed in a defined groupA controlled study in patients with a recognised case definition supports it.
3Repeated by othersAn independent group has reproduced the finding in a separate {{ gloss("cohort") }}.
4Tested by interventionChanging the proposed factor changes the outcome in a controlled experiment, so the link goes beyond association.
5Causal case builtSeveral independent lines of evidence, including experimental evidence, together meet most of the {{ gloss("Bradford Hill criteria") }}, with no unresolved major contradiction.

A treatment that works is one kind of experimental evidence and can count towards level 4. It does not, on its own, confirm the root cause: a treatment can help without acting on the cause. Level 5 requires the whole causal case, not a treatment result.

What moves a hypothesis up or down

Up: a new study meeting the criteria of the next rung; an independent {{ gloss("replication") }}; a better-defined cohort; a larger sample; a controlled design with a comparison group; a {{ gloss("preregistered") }} protocol.

Down or held back: failed replications; a finding that rests only on a broad case definition (Oxford-only cohorts) or a very small sample; no control group; no preregistration; results that conflict with equally strong studies (see "Contested evidence" below). A retracted study is removed from the evidence and the level is re-assessed.

Volume is not evidence. The number of papers, citations or news items never moves a hypothesis. Quality factors applied at every rung: case definition, sample size, controls, preregistration, conflicting results.

Accepted case definitions

Every study on a card records the case definition used to decide who counted as a patient.

How each case definition is treated

{{ gloss("Fukuda criteria", "Fukuda") }}Accepted, flagged on the card as "does not require PEM".

The two badges

Shown on every card, separate from the bar, each yes or no.

  1. Diagnostic marker validated in an independent cohort. Yes only when a measurable marker linked to the hypothesis has been tested in a second, independent patient group, with a stated case definition.
  2. Explains post-exertional malaise ({{ gloss("PEM") }}). Yes only when at least one study at level 2 or above measures PEM directly (for example a repeated exercise test on two consecutive days, or a controlled exertion challenge with symptoms tracked afterwards) and links it to the hypothesis. The link must connect the proposed mechanism to the symptoms after exertion in the same patients; a measured change that is itself the hypothesised abnormality (for example a fall in oxygen use on the second test) does not on its own explain PEM. A cohort of ME/CFS patients alone is not enough.

Contested evidence

A level is contested when at least one study of comparable or higher quality contradicts the finding that supports it. Comparable quality means all three: the same or a stricter case definition (CCC, ICC and IOM 2015 rank above Fukuda and Holmes 1988, which rank equal and above Oxford), a control group, and at least half the sample size of the study it contradicts. Sample size here means the number of patients in the measurement that bears on the finding, not the whole study when only part of it was measured. A rung that rests on more than one study is contested when a study of comparable quality contradicts any study the rung needs.

  • Who decides: the AI auditor (see "How content gets onto the site"), with the contradicting study linked on the card and the reason written in plain sentences, split into short paragraphs with their own sources when it needs more than two. The drafting agent may propose a conflict; it is set only when the auditor accepts it.
  • How it is shown: the bar is filled up to the highest uncontested rung; the rung or rungs under dispute are drawn as a hatched segment. The card lists both sides.
  • How it ends: a contested mark is removed when the conflict is resolved (for example a larger study settles it). The removal is recorded on What changed.

How content gets onto the site

No person decides the content of a card. AI agents draft it, a second agent reviews it, code checks every citation, and an independent AI auditor decides every level, badge, contested mark, layer, link and preprint signal. Every card shown here was audited as a whole card by two independent auditor runs before it was published, most of them after one or more correction rounds. The audit records are in the public repository.

The weekly flow

The pipeline runs on AI, with mechanical checks in code. The founder sets the direction and the rubric. This is the path of a weekly update to a card that is already published. How a card is first published, corrected or given new links is described below the table.

  1. Weekly fetch. New papers are fetched from PubMed and Europe PMC and the raw responses stored.
  2. Drafting agent. One AI agent sorts papers by hypothesis, extracts study data (sample size, case definition) and writes a draft card update. Each AI output keeps the source id it came from. The output is a draft only.
  3. Mechanical checks (code, not AI). Every cited id must exist in a fetched record; title, year and the case-definition quote are matched against that record; sample sizes are checked as a warning; every block of text must cite a source. A draft that fails is sent back to the drafting agent. No AI verdict overrides a failed check.
  4. Review agent. A second AI agent checks the draft against the sources, the inclusion rule and the ladder. It sees the draft and the source records, not the drafting agent's reasoning.
  5. Second round. In a weekly update, if the review agent rejects the draft, the drafting agent gets one revised attempt, which is checked again. If it is rejected again, the change leaves the card unchanged and is listed as a dispute. It is never sent to the auditor: no AI step overrules a rejection by the review agent. The founder may overrule it, as a logged correction.
  6. Audit. For a change of tier 2 (below), an independent AI auditor accepts it, rejects it, or accepts only a lower level. It is shown only the proposed change, the card as it stands, the cached source records the change cites (the full text of every source that carries a level, a badge or the contested mark, the abstract of the others), and the rubric on this page. It is not shown the drafting agent's reasoning, the review agent's findings or any earlier audit. It writes no card text. Every part of its decision names the rubric clause it rests on, and every factual statement in its reasons carries a source id and a quote that code checks against the cached record. When the auditor does not accept a change, the card stays as it is. When it accepts only a lower level, the drafting agent writes one redraft at that level, which goes through the checks, the review and a fresh audit (with a second auditor) again; it is applied only if every step accepts, and there is no further round.
  7. Consistency check. In a weekly update, for every level change, and one in five of the other audited changes, a second auditor with a fresh context decides the same change. If its verdict differs, the card stays as it is, the difference is treated as a dispute and it is counted. The count is reported in the description of each weekly update, which is a pull request in the public repository. It is not shown on this site.
  8. Publication. A change is applied only when the checks passed, the review agent accepted it and, for tier 2, the auditor accepted it. The card shows when it was last audited and by whom.
  9. Logging. Every published change gets an entry on What changed, with its date, card, reason, source and the tier. The log is public and kept permanently.
  10. Corrections. An error found later is corrected the same way and logged as a correction. If the founder overrules an auditor decision, that is a logged correction too.

First publication, corrections and added links

These rules are separate from the weekly path above.

  • First publication. Two independent auditor runs audit the whole card, one after the other. Both must accept it. If the first one rejects it, there is no second audit and the card is held back.
  • Correction round. A held-back card is not audited again as it is. A correction may only remove, weaken or correct what the review and the audits named. The one thing it may add is a single sentence that an audit asked for as a missing statement. That sentence may cite only the sources the audit quoted, and say no more than the quotes. The corrected card is reviewed again and then audited by two new auditor runs, which are not told that it was corrected.
  • Wording-only fixes. The review agent and the auditor mark each objection as decisive or as wording only. An objection is wording only when fixing it touches no level, badge, contested mark, layer, link, case definition, study role or number. In a correction round or a link round, the named sentence is then reworded or removed, and the review agent checks the fix once. If the fix does not resolve the objection, or adds a claim, the card is held back (in a link round, that link is dropped and the others go on). Any decisive objection takes the normal path.
  • Links added to a published card. Each link is decided on its own and needs two agreeing audits. A link round adds no study and does not change the card's layer.

Registered ongoing studies

The page of registered ongoing studies follows its own source rule, because a registry record is not a paper and is not evidence.

  • The only registry is ClinicalTrials.gov, read through its public programming interface. The raw responses are kept, and the rate limits are respected.
  • Every value about a study, on the page or in the downloadable data (registry number, title, study type, status, countries, planned enrolment, start date and the date the record was last updated), is copied by a program from the fetched record. Nothing about a study is written by a model or from memory.
  • A registered study is not evidence. It is never a study on a card, and it is never a source of a level, a badge, a contested mark, a layer or a link. It changes no card.
  • The only AI judgement is which card or cards a study bears on: a study is matched to a card only when it measures, tests or intervenes on what the card's core claim says. A second AI agent checks every match before it is shown. A match that fails the check is not shown. Before the page was first published, the checking step was run on all ongoing ME/CFS studies and on a sample of ongoing long COVID studies, and the page is shown only because at most one match in five was rejected.
  • The list states facts. It does not recommend that anyone take part, it names no contact person, and it says nothing about whether an intervention works.

Data resources

The page of data resources follows its own source rule, because a resource is not a paper and is not evidence.

  • A resource is listed when it holds data or samples from people with ME/CFS, researchers outside the owning group can get access to it, and a public page says how. Registers of research projects, groups or publications are listed separately. Resources for long COVID alone are left out; a resource that covers long COVID is included only when its page expressly covers both.
  • The source of an entry is the resource's own public page, stored with its address and the date it was fetched. A resource described only in a journal article is not listed. The fetch reads the site's robots rules first, sends a generic client name and never works around a bot check or a login. When a host refuses an automated request, the source is instead a copy of the page that the founder saved by hand from a browser, stored with its address and the date it was saved and marked as saved by hand; the same checks apply to it. A resource whose page can be neither fetched nor saved this way is not shown.
  • An entry has only fixed-value fields, a name and links. Every value carries a quote from the stored page, in the page's own language. A value the page does not state is not shown; it is never filled in from memory or from a candidate list.
  • An AI agent proposes the values and quotes. A program checks that every quote is found in the stored page, and a second AI agent judges each value on its own: does its quote support it. A value that fails is not shown (a rejected kind of content is dropped, and a register's rejected access is not shown), and no agent rewrites a value. An entry is left out when its name, kind, coverage or, for data and samples, its access does not hold. Before the page was first published, at most one value in five was rejected in a test of the whole set.
  • A resource is not evidence. It is never a study on a card, and it changes no level, badge, contested mark, layer or link. The page recommends nothing, and it is not an invitation to take part in or sign up to a resource. This site holds no patient data.

The role of AI, and who decides

AI drafts, checks and audits. Every evidence level, badge, contested mark and link on this site is decided by AI tools, and no medical or research expert has reviewed the content. The founder sets the rubric, the scope and the direction, and may overrule an auditor decision as a logged correction.

The auditor is an AI agent of the same kind as the agents it audits and may share their blind spots. What makes it independent is what it is shown, not the model it runs on. The second audit of a sample of weekly decisions measures how often two runs disagree.

Known limits

  • No medical or research expert has reviewed the content or the ladder yet.
  • The auditor is an AI agent and may share the blind spots of the agents whose work it audits.
  • The six rungs and their names have not been tested with readers.
  • Cards are only as complete as the searches run so far. The weekly search is started by hand, not on a schedule.
